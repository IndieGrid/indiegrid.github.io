What “days late” means here

An overdue invoice is easier to chase when the count of days and any fee come from a rule you can point at. This calculator takes the invoice amount, the due date, today’s date, a grace period, and one fee rule you type: a monthly percent, an annual percent, or a flat fee. It reports how many days count and what that rule produces. It does not decide whether your contract, or the law where you work, allows the fee.

Calendar days past due are today’s date minus the due date. Days late, the number that enters the fee, are those days minus the grace period, and never less than zero. A grace period of seven days means a bill that is five days past due still has a fee of zero. The same bill ten days past due has three days late. If today is still on or before the due date, both counts are zero.

The fee is simple interest, not compound interest. A month is taken as 30 days and a year as 365 days so you can reproduce the result without a spreadsheet. Those divisors are conventions of this page, not a statutory definition of a month.

monthly percent: fee = amount × (percent ÷ 100) × (days late ÷ 30)
annual percent: fee = amount × (percent ÷ 100) × (days late ÷ 365)
flat fee: if days late is at least 1, fee = the flat amount you typed; otherwise fee = 0

Only one rule runs at a time. If a contract adds a flat recovery amount on top of interest, add that yourself. This form does not stack them.

Worked example

The invoice is 960, due 1 November 2026, and today is 15 November 2026. Grace is 0. The rule is 8% a year.

days past due = 14
days late = 14
fee = 960 × 0.08 × (14 ÷ 365) = 2.95

Put the grace period at 7 and days late fall to 7. Because the annual rule is proportional to days, the fee is 960 × 0.08 × (7 ÷ 365) = 1.47. Switch to a flat fee of 40 and, once days late is at least 1, the fee is 40 with no daily accrual. Switch to 2% a month with no grace and the fee is 960 × 0.02 × (14 ÷ 30) = 8.96. Use the rule written in the agreement you actually have.

Late-fee rules are not the same everywhere. A contract may set them, a local law may limit them, and a client may pay the invoice while disputing the fee. In the European Union, the Late Payment Directive is a general framework for interest and a fixed recovery amount on certain business-to-business late payments. Whether that framework applies, and which rate it produces, depends on the country and the contract. This page does not look up a central-bank rate, does not add a standard recovery amount, and is not a statement of what you may charge. When the sum matters, read the contract or ask a qualified person where you work.
