Why a rate needs a year, not a vibe

Dividing a desired salary by 2,080 hours assumes you can bill every working hour of a 40-hour week, and it ignores both the costs of the work and any money you intend to reserve. Freelance weeks are smaller than that. Proposals, bookkeeping, and weeks you take off are not billed, and a set-aside that exists only as a good intention gets spent.

This calculator starts from an identity you can check on paper. The set-aside is a percentage of revenue, the same convention as the set-aside guide on this site, not a percentage of profit.

take-home = revenue − business expenses − (revenue × set-aside percent ÷ 100)

Rearranged, the revenue you need is:

revenue = (target take-home + business expenses) ÷ (1 − set-aside percent ÷ 100)

If you reserve 25%, only 75 cents of each dollar of revenue remains for expenses and take-home. Dividing by 0.75 is what makes the reserve real. Skipping that division, and only adding a rough buffer after you have already picked a rate, is how the reserve ends up too small.

Hours are the other half. A rate built on a week you cannot sell will not fund the year.

weeks worked = 52 − weeks off
effective billable hours per week = hours you entered × (1 − non-billable percent ÷ 100)
billable hours in the year = effective hours per week × weeks worked
hourly rate = revenue ÷ billable hours in the year
day rate = hourly rate × (effective hours per week ÷ 5)
monthly revenue target = revenue ÷ 12

The day rate is one fifth of an effective billable week, not a fixed eight-hour day. If the form says you can bill 24 hours in a week, a day is 4.8 of those hours. Non-billable time is where admin, sales, and rewriting a scope go. Leave that percent at zero only when those hours truly do not exist. The form stops you at 100% non-billable and at 52 weeks off, because both leave no hours to divide by. It also stops a set-aside of 100% or more, because nothing would remain for take-home or expenses.

Worked example

Target take-home 60,000. Business expenses 6,000. Set-aside 25%. You named 30 hours a week, four weeks off, and 20% of those hours non-billable.

weeks worked = 48
effective hours per week = 30 × 0.80 = 24
hours in the year = 24 × 48 = 1,152
revenue = (60,000 + 6,000) ÷ 0.75 = 88,000
hourly rate = 88,000 ÷ 1,152 = 76.39
day rate = 76.39 × (24 ÷ 5) = 366.67
monthly revenue target = 88,000 ÷ 12 = 7,333.33

The large figures on the page are rounded to the cent. The day rate is computed from the unrounded hourly number, then rounded for display. A fixed-price job you expect to take 20 billable hours needs about 20 × 76.39 in revenue before you discount it. If the pipeline is thin, raise the rate or the hours you can sell. Lowering the non-billable percent in the form, while the admin work stays, only hides the shortfall.

This is planning arithmetic for a year you describe. It is not a tax calculation, and it does not know what a client will accept. The Invoice + Money Tracker is where sent invoices and paid dates show whether the monthly target is actually arriving.
